Motivating dashboards for Gleanmo

— five directions, mocked with realistic data

Your pullups spreadsheet worked because of one property almost no tracking app has: it told you a number you had not done yet. Total done, running average, required average from today forward, remaining. Three of those four are backward-looking and only one of them — required pace — moves when you do nothing. That is the stat that pulls, because doing nothing is an action with a visible cost. Everything below is built on that observation.

All five mockups share one seeded data generator, so every number is internally consistent: the required-pace figure on one page matches the burn-up on another. "Today" is 2026-09-10 — day 253, 112 left. Nine practices, five different units. Nothing here touches source files; these are standalone HTML.

The five pitches

Mockup 01

The Pace Board

Your spreadsheet, rebuilt, for nine things at once — and with the one column the spreadsheet never had.

Hero stat
Required pace from today. Four tiles reproduce the spreadsheet exactly (total done / running average / required from today / remaining), and the burn-up chart draws three lines: what you did, the even-pace diagonal, and a bright ray from where you are now to the goal on Dec 31. The gap between the last two is the feeling.
The new column
Required pace, day by day — what the required number was on every past day. A rising line means you have been spending buffer. This is the chart that makes a slow slide visible in week two instead of month four, and no spreadsheet tab of yours had it.
Why it motivates
It converts "I skipped today" into a concrete price you can read tomorrow morning. And it never lies in the flattering direction: percent-complete looks great in November when it is hopeless; required pace goes vertical.
Generalizer
Gear = required pace ÷ current pace. Dimensionless, so 175 sessions, 6,000 pages and 100 hours become directly comparable: 1.00× = keep going, 2.16× = music needs to double. One sortable column ranks nine goals in five units.
Needs
A goal entity. Nothing else.

Mockup 02

The Closer

A phone screen you open at 9pm. No chart you have to interpret — just the cheapest thing you could still do tonight and exactly what it buys.

Hero stat
Distance to the nearest reachable threshold, ranked by minutes required. Every row is an ask with a price tag: "Draw: 24 minutes — you're 49 days behind; skipping tonight pushes tomorrow's number to 23.9/day."
Why it motivates
This is the "3 more and I hit 7,000" mechanic, industrialised. The spreadsheet pulled you in because a number was within today's reach. A dashboard that shows nine annual totals does not do that; a screen that sorts by cost and puts the 10-minute win at the top does. The Urgent block on top is the only place negative framing is allowed — a streak that dies at midnight is genuinely unrecoverable, unlike everything else on the page.
Underrated bit
The hour-of-day band. "You still have time" is a claim, so the page proves it from your own logs: 29% of a typical day's activity happens after 7pm. And Already banked restates what you did today in the page's own currency ("bought 2.1 days of pace") so the visit ends on a credit, not a to-do list.
Needs
Goals, plus a per-practice minimum dose. Both are goal-entity fields.

Mockup 03

The Grid

Nine practices, one shared date axis. A vertical slice is a day of your life.

Hero stat
The chain. The longest streak still alive, drawn as links, with the empty links to your personal record shown at the same size. 16 filled, 43 empty, record 59. Beating your own number is the only competition this app can honestly offer, so it should be drawn literally.
Why it motivates
Streaks are the cheapest motivation you can build (no goal-setting required) and the most fragile. Showing distance-to-record next to distance-to-zero keeps a broken streak from being a total loss — the record is still there to chase.
Why one axis
Nine separate calendar heatmaps answer "how was drawing?". One aligned grid answers "what did a good fortnight look like?" — the July collapse hits drawing, writing and music in the same two weeks, and you can only see that if the rows line up. Deliberately not ECharts' calendar coordinate system, which is one calendar per chart.
Diagnostics
The atrophy radar (last 30 days vs. the 30 before) names the practice quietly dying while annual totals still look fine. The day-of-week grid turns "I'm bad at music" into "music on Sundays is 14%" — a schedule problem you fix once, not a willpower problem you fight weekly.
Needs
Nothing new for the grid itself. Weekly-minimum streaks want one habit field.

Mockup 04

The Ledger

The other half of the spreadsheet feeling: the satisfaction of having gone past a number. Almost no charts, on purpose.

Hero stat
Lifetime odometers — 537 workouts, 207,011 words, 1,967 hours of software. Numbers that only ever go up, animated on load like a mechanical counter. These are the ones you read when you are tired and pace math would only make you feel worse.
Why it motivates
Pace pulls; evidence consoles. A motivation system that only ever pushes forward is exhausting, and the day you fall behind on everything is exactly the day you stop opening it. The Ledger is the page that still has something good to say on that day — which is what makes the whole system survivable.
Crossed / Next up
Both derived, nothing stored: a crossing date is just the first day the running total exceeded a threshold. "71 thresholds crossed so far in 2026" is a real sentence you can generate from data you already have, and Next up is the same list one step forward, ordered by ETA at current rate.
Records
Best day / week / month / longest streak per practice, with recent records marked gold. A record you are currently near is worth far more than one from 2023, so the page says which is which.
Needs
Nothing new. Every number is a fold over logs you already write.

Mockup 05

The Ghost

Race the only opponent you have: the person who logged all of this last year.

Hero stat
Days ahead of last year. Your 2026 cumulative curve against 2025's, with 2025 drawn all the way to Dec 31 so you can see the finish line you have to beat. "Last year you didn't reach 122 workouts until Nov 12."
Why it motivates
A goal is a number you picked, and you know you picked it — so missing it is negotiable. Last year is a number you actually hit. Falling behind it stings in a way an aspirational target never does, and passing it is unambiguous. It also solves the cold-start problem: this page works before you have set a single goal.
Best chart on any of these pages
The gap — the running difference between this year and last, green above zero. It names the exact week you pulled ahead, which is a stretch worth deliberately copying rather than mythologising.
Also
Rolling 90 days ranks right now against every 90-day window you have ever had ("#111 of 529, 3 sessions from your record"), and the diverging bar chart puts all nine practices vs. their own ghost on one screen — that compressed version is the strongest candidate for the actual home page.
Needs
Nothing. Just history — which you now have, since the port.

What generalizes, and what has to stay specific

Every one of your nine activities is already either an interval log, a point log, or a habit log. That means all nine reduce to the same shape:

;; the whole abstraction
{:series  [0 1 0 0 2 1 ...]   ; one number per day, in the user's time zone
 :unit    :sessions            ; sessions | minutes | days | pages | words
 :goal    175
 :window  ["2026-01-01" "2026-12-31"]}

Everything on all five mockups is a pure function of that map. Total, running average, required pace, gear, remaining, projected finish date, days ahead/behind, current streak, longest streak, weekly coverage, rolling-90 rank, milestone distance, crossing dates, ghost comparison, personal bests. One namespace computes all of it, nine times, and a tenth practice costs nothing. That is the argument for building the engine before building any specific screen.

Only three transformations vary by entity type, and they are small:

  • Interval logs (meditation, reading, project, exercise-session, boulder-session): series = summed minutes per day, or count of intervals per day.
  • Point logs (habit-log, mood-log, bm-log): series = count per day, usually 0/1.
  • Child rollups (exercise-line → set → session): series = summed reps × weight, or count.

What genuinely cannot generalize, and should live on a per-domain screen instead of being forced into the engine:

  • Exercise: tonnage, per-exercise PRs, muscle balance, rest intervals. Your richest data by far, and none of it fits a single daily number.
  • Reading: "% through this book" and books finished. Progress against a bounded target is a different feeling from progress against an annual quota — a book you are 84 pages from finishing is the strongest distance-to-threshold stat in your whole dataset, and the year-total view cannot express it.
  • Bouldering: grade pyramid, send rate per grade. Ordinal, not additive.
  • Winddown: it is three sub-actions (brush, floss, skincare), so "done" is a completeness question, not a count.
  • Mood: correlations with practice. Interesting, but informative, not motivating — keep it off the hero screens. "You meditate more on high-stress days" changes nothing you do tonight.

Schema proposals, ranked by payoff

1. A goal entity — the actual unlock

Without this, every target on every mockup is a magic number in Clojure. With it, all five pages are generic over any entity you ever add. This is the single change that makes the difference between "a dashboard" and "a dashboard system", and it is worth building before any screen.

(def goal
  [:map {:closed true}
   [:xt/id :goal/id]
   [::sm/type [:enum :goal]]
   [::sm/created-at :instant]
   [::sm/deleted-at {:optional true} :instant]
   [:user/id :user/id]
   [:goal/label {:crud/priority 1} :string]
   ;; what is being counted, and from where
   [:goal/source-type {:crud/priority 2}   ; the entity the series comes from
    [:enum :exercise-session :meditation-log :reading-log
           :project-log :habit-log :boulder-session]]
   [:goal/metric {:crud/priority 3}
    [:enum :occurrences :minutes :days-active :output]]
   ;; optional narrowing: one project, one habit, one book
   [:goal/scope-ids {:optional true, :crud/priority 4} [:set :uuid]]
   ;; the target
   [:goal/target {:crud/priority 5} :number]
   [:goal/period {:crud/priority 6}
    [:enum :day :week :month :year :open]]   ; :open = the 10k-pullups shape
   [:goal/starts-on {:crud/priority 7} :local-date]
   [:goal/ends-on {:optional true, :crud/priority 8} :local-date]
   ;; the smallest ask that still counts — drives The Closer's ranking
   [:goal/minimum-dose {:optional true, :crud/priority 9} :number]
   [:goal/archived {:optional true} :boolean]])

Two things worth defending. :goal/period :open exists because your best-ever goal was not annual — 10,000 pullups had a start and no calendar, and forcing it into "per year" would lose the shape that worked. And :goal/minimum-dose looks like UI config, but it is the number that makes The Closer rank honestly; without it every ask costs "one session" and the sort is meaningless.

Note it carries no :goal/streak, :goal/current-total or :goal/last-computed-at. Those are folds over logs, and a stored fold is a cache with an invalidation bug in its future — the log is edited retroactively (which you do) and the number silently stops matching.

2. project/category — the cheapest fix with the biggest reach

[:project/category {:optional true, :crud/priority 3,
                    :crud/suggest-existing true} :string]

Five of your nine practices are projects, and two of them (software, creative) are several projects each. Right now a "creative time this week" number means hardcoding a list of project ids that goes stale the moment you start a new video. A category string with :crud/suggest-existing — the same pattern boulder-problem/gym already uses, and for the same reason — makes the rollup a query instead of a constant, and it costs one optional field and about ten minutes of backfill.

I'd take :string over an enum here deliberately: your set of creative practices is exactly the kind of thing that changes without warning, and an enum would need a schema edit each time.

3. project-log/output-count + output-unit — the "did I make something" number

[:project-log/output-count {:optional true, :crud/priority 5} :number]
[:project-log/output-unit  {:optional true, :crud/priority 6,
                            :crud/suggest-existing true} :string]  ; words, pages, panels, bars, commits

Duration rewards sitting there. That is fine for meditation, where the sitting is the practice, and actively bad for writing, where 90 minutes of staring produces the same green cell as 90 minutes of drafting. Your pullups goal worked partly because reps cannot be faked by presence.

I'd argue for one generic pair rather than writing/word-count, drawing/piece-count, and so on. A single pair means one chart serves writing, drawing, music and software; per-domain fields mean a new schema, converter, formatter and chart every time you pick up a practice. The unit varies per project, not per log, so a relation default (see roadmap/080-relation-defaults.md) can prefill it and the field costs one number at stop-timer time.

Honest risk: it adds friction to a flow that is currently one tap, and a field you skip half the time produces a chart that lies. Mitigation is that output is optional and never replaces duration — the practice still counts as done without it; the output number just unlocks a second, better goal for the practices where it exists.

4. Reading — book/page-count first, reading-log/end-page second

[:book/page-count        {:optional true, :crud/priority 6} :int]
[:reading-log/end-page   {:optional true, :crud/priority 4} :int]

You floated page counts, so here is the argument for a narrower version than roadmap/055 proposes. That doc's Option 1 is start-page + end-page; I'd ship end-page only. Start page is derivable — it is the previous log's end page for the same book — so the second field doubles the friction to recover a number you already have. The exception (you skipped pages) is rare enough to handle by editing the record.

And I'd rank book/page-count above the log field, which 055 does not mention at all. Pages-per-hour is a nice statistic; "84 pages from finishing" is a threshold you can cross tonight, and it needs the book's length, not the session's. It is the one place in your whole dataset with a naturally bounded target — every other goal is an arbitrary annual quota, and finishing a book is not.

Note what it can't do: audiobooks. Given reading-log/format already exists, pages should just be absent for audio sessions and the progress bar should say so rather than guess.

5. habit/target-per-week — one int, and streaks stop lying

[:habit/target-per-week {:optional true, :crud/priority 3} :int]

A daily streak is the wrong metric for anything you do 4×/week — one honest rest day reads as failure, which trains you to log dishonestly. With an expected cadence, "week met" becomes the streak unit and the number stays truthful. It also gives The Closer its Thursday-night sentence: "3 days left in the week and 2 sessions still owed."

For winddown specifically, I would not add a routine/parent-habit structure. One habit called "Winddown", logged once, with brush/floss/skincare as its definition, gets you every stat on these pages. Three sub-habits get you a completeness percentage you will not act on differently, and a schema relationship that has to be maintained forever.

6. Exercise metadata — better payoff than page counts

[:exercise/movement-pattern {:optional true, :crud/suggest-existing true} :string]
[:exercise/primary-region   {:optional true, :crud/suggest-existing true} :string]

You asked which additions would actually pay off. This one has the best ratio on the list, and it is not in your roadmap as a schema change — 046-exercise-insights.md assumes the muscle heatmap is a rendering problem when it is a data problem.

The reason it is cheap: these go on exercise, an entity, not on exercise-line, a log. You fill in a few dozen rows once, and every set you have ever logged and will ever log inherits the classification retroactively. Compare with output-count, which taxes every future session and does nothing for the past. What it unlocks: the muscle heatmap 046 wants, push/pull balance, "you have not trained a hinge in 19 days" (a distance-to-threshold stat for a thing you would otherwise never notice), and per-pattern PR tracking.

Things I would not add

  • Per-session quality or satisfaction ratings. Friction on every log, and the resulting series is untrustworthy in a specific way: as you improve, your standards rise, so a genuine upward trend in skill shows as a flat or falling satisfaction line. A motivation dashboard that punishes improvement is worse than no dashboard. (If you want it for reflection, it belongs in notes, not a charted field.)
  • Materialized streak / total / rank fields. Derive them. The <entity>/running flag is the precedent people will cite, but that one is legitimate because it is current state with a sparse index, not a fold over history — and your own AGENTS.md already warns against inventing composite attributes that fake an index the engine lacks. A streak is exactly that temptation.
  • A separate drawing-log / writing-log entity per practice (one of the options in 030-drawing-practice.md). Five near-identical schemas, five CRUD registrations, five sets of viz routes — and the moment you add a sixth practice you do it all again. project-log plus a category plus an optional output count covers all of them, and the generic engine then treats a new practice as data entry rather than a feature.

Charting notes — where ECharts earns it, and where it doesn't

ECharts 6 is already loaded on demand via the ::ui/echarts ctx flag, so using it costs nothing extra. It's the right tool for five of the charts here: the burn-up with its three lines and goal markLine, the required-pace history, the ghost race and gap, the rolling-90 window, the atrophy radar, and the trophy scatter. All of them are multi-series, hover-worthy, and want a real axis.

Four things on these mockups deliberately are not ECharts, each for a reason:

  • The Grid's 9 × 253 wall — hand-rolled SVG. ECharts' calendar coordinate system gives you one calendar per chart; the entire point of this view is nine rows on a shared axis. Nine stacked charts would drift on resize and could never share a tooltip.
  • The Closer's ring and progress arcs — inline SVG with stroke-dasharray. A single-value gauge does not need a chart engine, and the arc animates from a CSS transition for free.
  • The Ledger's odometers — CSS transforms on stacked digits. Canvas text cannot be selected, does not respect the user's font settings, and blurs when you zoom. Numbers this important should be real text.
  • Progress bars and pace tracks — plain divs with tabular-nums. They reflow with the table, print, and cost nothing.

One genuine gap in ECharts for this use: there is no good built-in "bullet chart" (actual vs. target vs. required on one thin row). The Pace Board's ledger table fakes it with a div and a tick mark, which is honestly better than what a chart library would produce at that size.

What I'd build first

Build The Pace Board — but build the goal entity and the series engine underneath it first.

It is the direct descendant of the only system that has ever actually worked for you, which is the strongest evidence available and better than any argument I can make from design principles. It also produces the most reusable substrate: once (practice-series ctx goal) → {:series :total :pace :required :gear :streak ...} exists, mockups 02, 04 and 05 are view layers over the same map. None of them needs new data access. That sequencing is what keeps this from becoming five separate features.

Order I'd go in:

  1. The goal entity + the series engine. Nine goals covering the nine practices. Follow the scan-then-pull rule in db/queries.clj: an index-only [?e ?timestamp] scan bounded by the goal window, then fetch-entities-by-ids. The daily fold happens in Clojure, not Datalog.
  2. The Pace Board. Tabs, four tiles, burn-up, the ranked ledger table. Required-pace history can come in a second pass.
  3. The Ghost's diverging bar — the nine-practice "vs. last year" strip — dropped onto the existing overview page. Half a day's work, needs no new schema, and it is the single most motivating thing per line of code on this whole page.
  4. The Closer, once minimum-dose exists. This is the one you will actually open daily, but it is worth less before the goal data is trustworthy — a ranked list built on guessed targets ranks wrong.
  5. The Ledger and The Grid, in either order. Both are pure reads over existing data and can land whenever.

Two smaller notes worth acting on regardless of which page wins. First, project/category is worth adding before the goal entity even though it is not required by it — five of your nine goals will want to scope to a category, and adding it after means editing goals you just made. Second, if you build only one thing from this entire page, make it the required-pace-from-today number, on the existing overview, for one practice. That single number is the whole thesis, and if it does not pull you the way the spreadsheet did, none of the rest is worth building.
